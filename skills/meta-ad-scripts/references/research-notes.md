# Research notes — Meta short-form video ad scripts

Working document. Raw findings from web research (blogs, Reddit-adjacent communities, X/LinkedIn posts from named ad creators, YouTube-indexed creator content, agency breakdowns). Distilled into the skill in `SKILL.md` / `references/*.md`. Every principle in the skill traces back to something here.

## 1. Script structures

**Baseline (everywhere):** Hook → Problem → Solution → CTA, and its close cousins:
- Hook → Problem → Product/Solution → Proof → CTA (5-part; "best-performing UGC ad structure" per multiple ad-agency sources)
- AIDA: Attention → Interest → Desire → Action
- PAS: Problem → Agitate → Solution
- BAB: Before → After → Bridge
- Hook → Educate (overdeliver value/trust) → CTA (3-part "value ad" framework, common in YouTube ad scripting circles)
- WHY/WHAT/HOW: open with problem via curiosity/contrarian hook → agitate the pain they've been ignoring → pivot to "the mechanism" (what makes the solution different), teasing it without fully revealing it → CTA

**Named alternative structures found in research:**
- **"Tested alternatives" / skeptic format** — "I tried [X products/tools/brands]" hook → quick dismissal of alternatives tried → introduce product with a clear differentiating reason → one concrete specific result → direct CTA. Explicitly called out as handling skepticism directly: "I was skeptical about [product] because [common doubt]. But after [timeframe], [specific result]. The thing that surprised me most? [unexpected benefit]. If you [audience problem], just try it — [CTA]."
- **Myth-busting** — state a common false belief the audience holds → debunk it with evidence/proof (ideally via an authority/credible POV — "authority bias") → reposition product as the belief-correct solution. Appeals to viewers' desire to feel smarter/in-the-know. Risk: can read condescending if not handled carefully — educate, don't lecture.
- **Contrarian / hot take** — "Why [common practice] is [wrong/costing you]" template, e.g. "Why your A/B tests are giving you the wrong answer." Targets problem-aware skeptics who've tried the standard solution already and are resistant to standard messaging. Needs a genuinely defensible position with proof, not just a spicy claim — but if it lands, disproportionate attention.
- **Before/After (visual transformation)** — establish relatable "before" state (full of imperfections) → contrast with "after" state achieved via the product. Best suited to visually-provable transformations (beauty, home, fitness); can also run as pure BAB narration.
- **Day-in-the-life** — better suited to warm audiences / brand-building rather than hard conversion; the creator isn't "selling," the product is woven naturally into a routine. Template: "Come with me for my [routine]. First, [step]. Then — okay, this is where [product] comes in. [Natural usage + one benefit line]. Honestly it's the [superlative] part of my day. [rest of routine]. [soft CTA]." Rarely works past ~45s in a paid placement.
- **Testimonial** — customer speaks directly to camera about their experience; handles skepticism directly through lived-experience proof rather than claims.
- **"They don't want you to know" / secret-angle** — "What [authority/industry] doesn't want you to know about [topic]" — opens a curiosity gap (promises info without giving it away), appeals to feeling like an insider. Violates pattern expectations, which is why attention holds.
- **Question → twist → reveal** — open on a pattern-interrupting question/surprising visual/bold claim (0–3s) → build tension, delay the payoff ("cut on the question, not the answer" — a rule borrowed from serialized TV writing: if something surprising just happened, cut before the explanation) → deliver the reveal/payoff only once trust in the payoff is earned.
- **Harmon Brothers formula** — sound direct-response sales principles executed through comedic, branded delivery; humor is in service of the sales structure, not the goal itself ("humor is great, but it's not the goal"). One spokesperson reflecting the buyer's demographic, with comedy "sprinkled in," built around a 7-step sales formula. Explicitly reject chasing virality as a goal — a script should be built to sell, and if it also goes viral that's a bonus, not the target.

**Structure selection logic found in research:** structure choice should follow audience awareness level and goal — cold/unaware audiences respond to problem-first or myth-busting framing (they need the problem named for them); warm/previously-exposed audiences respond better to contrarian or "tested alternatives" framing (they've already heard the standard pitch); brand-building/retention goals suit day-in-the-life or testimonial; hard direct-response/conversion goals suit hook-problem-solution-proof-CTA or PAS.

## 2. Hooks

**What makes a hook scroll-stopping:**
- It must function as a **pattern interrupt** — the brain disengages from autopilot scrolling to resolve something that doesn't fit the expected pattern of the feed.
- Hooks are responsible for an outsized share of ad performance variance — one agency source states hooks drive 60–80% of performance variance; separately, tests show hook changes can produce 50–200% swings in video performance while body-content changes rarely exceed 20–30% impact. Practical implication: iterate hooks first and most aggressively.
- Ultra-short hook windows outperform: hooks lasting 0.5–1 second reportedly outperform 2–3 second intros by 2–3x (i.e., get to the interrupt/payoff immediately, don't "intro" your way in).
- A TikTok review of top-performing ads found 63% delivered their core message within the first three seconds.

**Data on hook types by audience awareness/skepticism (from research):**
- **Questions** — engage differently than statements because the brain automatically tries to answer them; best for problem-aware audiences; one stat cited: question-based hooks get ~4x more engagement than statement posts.
- **Stats/shocking numbers** — best for skeptical/analytical/data-driven audiences; numbers signal authority and credibility.
- **Counterintuitive statements / contrarian claims** — best for audiences who believe they already know the solution (need a perspective shift).
- **Pain-first hooks** — acknowledge current frustration/crisis directly.
- **Outcome-first hooks** — promise a specific result up front.
- Rule found repeatedly: **pick one hook type and commit — don't blend multiple hook mechanisms in the same opening line.**

**Named hook-formula lists found in research:**
- **Nick Theriot's 13 hook patterns** (X/LinkedIn, ad scaling coach): Big claim (e.g. "$10K/month w/ FB ads"), Fast claim ("Double budget overnight"), Authority ("I spent $10M on ads…"), Before/After ("1x → 3x ROAS"), Rival compare ("3x faster than XYZ"), No limits ("Even w/ no experience"), Question, Info, Newness, Exclusivity, Belief flip, Solution callout, Direct callout. (Full worked examples only partly recoverable via search — categories are the reusable part.) His broader method: keep a "swipe file" of hooks pulled from TikToks, Reels, Quora headlines and news, and iterate 80% on proven angles / 20% on genuinely new angles and personas.
- **Dara Denney (Meta ads creative strategist)**: call out the target demographic directly in the hook ("Calling all busy moms," "Makeup tips for 50+ skin") to self-select the right viewer; reaction-style hooks and trigger words; problem-solution hooks; single testimonial vertical UGC from creators who read as authentic on camera outperforms polished creative. She also flags that most operators over-index on the hook line itself and under-index on **energy** — the delivery energy in the hook and through the video is what actually evokes the emotion that drives clicks, not just the words.
- **"They don't want you to know"** authority/secret-angle formula (see structures above) — also functions as a standalone hook.

**Hook rate / hold rate data (Meta specifically, 2025):**
- Hook rate = 3-second video views ÷ impressions. Benchmarks found: ~20–25% hook rate / ~40–50% hold rate is solid for most accounts; 30–50% hook rates reachable once creative is optimized; 35–45% hook rate considered elite.
- Hold rate measures retention among those who passed the 3-second mark. High hook rate + hold rate under ~25% = a pacing problem in the body, not a hook problem — fix by tightening edits or adding another pattern interrupt around the 3-second mark, not by rewriting the hook again.
- Nearly half of viewers who stay past 3 seconds go on to watch to 30 seconds (Meta data point) — meaning the interrupt has to keep re-earning attention every few seconds, not just once at the very start.

## 3. Copywriting techniques

- **Pattern interrupt**: sudden break in the expected tone/pace/topic that stops the "autopilot" brain from tuning out; can be a bold/surprising claim, a reversal ("don't click unless…"), or an unrelated-seeming opening that resolves into relevance.
- **Open loops**: start a story, ask a question, or promise a reveal, then deliberately delay resolving it — the unresolved tension is what keeps someone watching. Must eventually be closed within the ad; never leave the loop open past the CTA or it reads as clickbait and erodes trust.
- **Specificity over vague claims**: "platitudes and generalities roll off the human understanding like water off a duck" (Claude Hopkins, classic direct-response principle still cited as foundational). Concrete numbers, timeframes, percentages, and named details read as more truthful and convert better than generic superlatives — vagueness signals a sales pitch, specificity signals a real account.
- **Emotional triggers over feature lists**: ~95% of buying decisions happen subconsciously/emotionally and get justified with logic afterward. Core triggers found repeatedly across sources: fear (of loss/missing out), identity/belonging (feeling part of a group), curiosity (information gaps), desire (a described better future), trust/safety. Identity-and-belonging framing tends to outperform copy that only states functional benefits.
- **Conversational register**: write at the reading/speaking level of a real conversation — contractions, short sentences, direct address ("you," "I," "we"), active voice. The explicit benchmark found in research: "write like you speak, only a little better." Read every script aloud before finalizing — awkward phrasing and anything that doesn't sound like natural speech shows up immediately when spoken.
- **Cut jargon and marketing-speak categorically** — if the audience needs a dictionary, they disengage; niche/brand jargon signals "this wasn't written for me."
- **Pacing/tonal shifts must be earned, not decorative** — a shift in tone needs a narrative reason and must be reinforced by pacing/visual cues, not dropped in arbitrarily.
- **Design for sound-off AND sound-on**: a large share of Facebook feed viewing happens muted (up to 85% cited for Facebook specifically), while Reels/Stories viewers are more likely to have sound on. Practical implication for spoken scripts: the spoken line has to carry the entire meaning on its own (since this skill outputs pure spoken text, not captions), but should still be written so that a caption rendering of it would land the point on the first read — short clauses, one idea per sentence, no meaning that depends on a later sentence to land.

## 4. Named experts — what they say

- **Nick Theriot** — ad-scaling coach, author of "The Art of Writing Facebook Ads That Scale." Hook-pattern-driven approach (13 patterns, see above); recommends building a hook swipe file from real-world sources (not inventing from scratch); 80/20 testing rule (mostly iterate proven angles, minority budget on genuinely new angles/personas); describes his scripting approach as starting from the text hook, then script, then supporting elements.
- **Dara Denney** — Meta ads creative strategist/consultant. Demographic call-outs in hooks; single-testimonial vertical UGC from authentic on-camera creators as a top-converting format; emphasis that hook *delivery energy*, not just hook wording, drives the emotional response that drives clicks; advises giving UGC creators fully-specified scripts rather than a loose brief, because creators are typically weak at inventing their own hooks.
- **Barry Hott** — "the ugly ads guy," known for deliberately unpolished/native-feeling creative. Core claim: a logo or branded polish shown too early loses the viewer immediately — native, low-fi presentation keeps the "this is an ad" pattern-recognition response from triggering. States he has re-filmed top-performing ads word-for-word, shot-for-shot with different people and had them fail — meaning script and angle alone don't guarantee results; delivery/energy/casting matters as much as the words. One case cited: shifting from polished to UGC-style drove a 72% ROAS increase and 90% revenue increase for a brand. Approach starts from deep audience empathy — think, speak, and consume like the customer before writing.
- **Harmon Brothers** — viral direct-response video ad agency (Squatty Potty, Purple, Chatbooks). Sound sales fundamentals executed with comedic, branded delivery; humor is a delivery vehicle for the sales structure, not the objective; explicitly warn against chasing "viral" as the goal — the goal is a script that sells, virality is a possible side effect, not the target; described process as a repeatable 7-step formula, one spokesperson reflecting the buyer's demographic, comedy "sprinkled in."
- **Alex Hormozi** (short-form/hook framing, cross-applies from YouTube Shorts advice to ad hooks) — first 3–5 seconds are everything; hook = "the sale before the sale," it earns the right to deliver the rest of the message; open with a bold statement, a surprising stat, or a pain-point question; one clear takeaway per piece of content, no rambling — rambling kills retention.

## 5. Common mistakes / what makes scripts generic

- Trying to say too much: ads fail "not because they're too simple, but because they try to say too much" — cramming in every feature/benefit instead of earning attention with one idea.
- Slow, "intro-y" openings — anything that delays the pattern interrupt past the first second or two measurably underperforms punchier, immediate openers.
- Generic messaging that tries to appeal to everyone: "ad copy that tries to appeal to everyone typically resonates with no one." Broad, hedge-everything promises read as weak and don't close skeptical viewers — specificity is what turns a browser into a buyer.
- Vague CTAs ("Learn More") instead of a single, concrete next action ("Get 20% off today," "Start your free trial").
- Overloaded openings/headlines trying to communicate everything at once.
- Losing brand/human voice to generic, robotic, AI-flattened phrasing — scripts that could be read by any brand about any product are a failure state.
- Excess modifiers/intensifiers and filler words: qualifiers and intensifiers, used past a point, dilute the core message and read as padding rather than persuasion.
- Generic, throat-clearing openers ("It's no secret that…", broad platitudes) — these are exactly the kind of sentence that must be cut ruthlessly since they delay the hook and add nothing.
- Showing branding/logo too early kills the native feel and triggers "this is an ad" pattern recognition, causing an immediate scroll-past.
- Mismatched script and delivery: even a proven winning script can fail with different delivery/casting — the words are necessary but not sufficient; but since this skill only produces the words, it should write scripts that are easy to deliver with natural, low-effort energy (short clauses, natural speech rhythm) rather than scripts that require a performance to rescue them.

## 6. Length and pacing

**Speaking pace:** natural conversational speech runs ~125–150 words per minute (~2.5 words/second); social/short-form delivery tends to run faster and more energetic, up to ~170+ wpm; a commonly cited practical rate for script-writing math is ~2.5–3 words per second.

**Word counts by duration (commercial/VO industry + short-form ad sources, ranges reconciled):**
- **15 seconds:** ~30–40 words
- **30 seconds:** ~70–90 words (aim to read in the 25–29s range to leave breathing/emphasis room)
- **60 seconds:** ~150–180 words

**Structural implication by duration:**
- **15s:** effectively hook + one core proof point + CTA — no room for a real "body," so the structure has to compress problem+solution into a single beat or skip straight from hook to proof to CTA. Best suited to: single testimonial, single before/after beat, quick myth-bust one-liner.
- **30s:** the "standard" ad length — enough room for hook → problem (brief) → solution/mechanism → one concrete proof point → CTA, or a full PAS/BAB pass. Most of the structures above are naturally 30s-shaped.
- **60s:** room for a real narrative arc — a full storytime/testimonial with a beginning-middle-end, a day-in-the-life, a myth-bust with more than one myth, or a Harmon-Brothers-style comedic build. Still needs the hook to land in the first 1–3 seconds; extra length is budget for the middle, not for a slower opening.

**Format-specific length/placement data (Meta specifically):** Feed video performs best at 15–30s; Stories/Reels best at 6–15s (native, fast-decision contexts); in-stream can stretch 15–120s. Within UGC specifically: testimonials and before/afters perform best at 15–30s; in-context use/unboxing at 20–40s; tutorials can run 45–60s only if the hook is strong enough to earn the extra time; day-in-the-life rarely works past ~45s in paid placement. Very short videos (1–9s bucket) actually had the *lowest* hold rate in one dataset — there is such a thing as too short; the floor isn't zero, it's "long enough to deliver one real idea."

## 7. Platform (Meta) specifics

- **Native beats polished.** Ads that look/feel native to the feed outperform visibly "produced" ads in most DTC categories — polished value cues (studio lighting, professional VO, lower-third graphics) trip the feed-trained viewer's ad-pattern-recognition and trigger an automatic scroll-past. One cited data point: native-format ads showed 35% higher view completion than non-native in matched-audience tests; UGC/testimonial-style reportedly performs 40–60% better than traditional product demos on Reels.
- **Sound behavior differs by placement.** Up to ~85% of Facebook feed video is watched muted; Instagram/Reels viewers are more likely to have sound on. Since this skill's output is spoken-word only (no caption design), the script must carry full meaning through the spoken line alone and be written so it would still land if only read as on-screen text — short, self-contained sentences, no meaning that depends on tone alone.
- **Placement shapes format and pacing, not just aspect ratio.** Feed = browsing mindset, scrolling quickly, needs the value proposition communicated immediately. Reels/Stories = fast binary decision (watch or skip) with viewers deciding almost instantly, so the hook has to be even more front-loaded than in Feed. In-stream allows more runway.
- **Algorithm shift toward creative-driven delivery (2026, "Project Andromeda").** Meta's ad delivery increasingly reads the creative itself to predict audience match rather than relying purely on targeting — meaning the quality and native-feel of the script/creative has become a more direct performance lever, not just a downstream creative-quality nicety.
- **Hook rate / hold rate as the operative Meta-specific metrics** (see section 2) — a script should be evaluated in terms of "will this survive the 3-second mark" and "will this keep giving people a reason to stay every few seconds after that," which is a Meta-specific framing (as opposed to, say, a YouTube pre-roll where skippability after 5s is the mechanic, or TikTok's fully algorithmic For You placement).
- **CTA placement:** primary CTA in the final 3–5s, reinforced verbally; multiple CTA touchpoints (early/mid/end) reportedly outperform a single end-CTA, but for short-form (15–30s) spoken scripts there usually isn't room for more than a soft internal nudge plus a clear final CTA line.
